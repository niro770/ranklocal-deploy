How to Use AI Call Scoring and Summaries to Coach Your Team and Track Jobs

Use transcripts and summaries to answer two questions: what happened on each call, and which calls are worth your time to hear. Score a small sample against a short rubric, coach from the results, and tie each call to a job outcome. Get legal advice on recording before you start, because consent rules vary by state.

Once you've decided to grow your home-service company, the office becomes the engine, and you can't coach what you can't see. AI summaries make call review faster. They don't make it automatic, and they don't remove the need to listen.

What you're getting from the software

Most tools in this category do some combination of the following, though features differ, so check what any one product actually does:

  • Turn a recording into a text transcript.
  • Write a short summary of the call: caller, problem, outcome.
  • Tag the call: new lead, existing customer, spam, wrong number, price shopper.
  • Score the call against questions you define.
  • Pull out keywords, such as a competitor's name or "how much."
  • Push the result into a CRM or spreadsheet.

You still need a way to capture the call in the first place. If you haven't set that up, start with call tracking for contractors, which covers numbers, recordings, and what to set up first.

Recording consent comes first

Before any recording, transcript, or scoring, settle the legal side. Laws on recording phone calls differ by state: some require consent from one party, others from everyone on the call, and the rules can change. Calls that cross state lines add questions. This is general information, not legal advice. Consult an attorney in your state about when you can record, what notice to give, how long to keep recordings, and who can access them.

A common practical step is a short recorded notice at the start of the call, but whether that's enough for your situation is exactly what the attorney should answer. Also ask about any transcription vendor's handling of the audio, since your callers' names, addresses, and phone numbers will sit in those transcripts.

Build a short scorecard

A rubric with 20 questions won't get used. Pick 6 to 8 things that predict whether a call becomes a job. The longer version, with what to listen for, is in how to score contractor sales calls. A compact version:

Item Yes/No What it shows
Answered within a few rings, with company name First impression
Asked for name, number and address early Intake discipline
Confirmed the job type and urgency Qualification
Handled price question without dodging Objection skill
Offered a specific appointment time Booking attempt
Confirmed the booking back to the caller Show rate
Tone: patient, not rushed Rapport

Give each item a yes or no. Add the yeses for a simple score out of seven. Resist weighted formulas until you've used the simple version for a month.

Software can fill in this scorecard for you, but check its work. Pull ten scored calls, listen, and compare your marks to the tool's. If it's often wrong on an item, drop that item from the automatic score and mark it yourself.

A weekly review routine

Aim for something you'll still be doing in six months.

  1. Pull the sample. Choose a few calls per CSR from the week. Include some booked, some not booked, and at least one long call.
  2. Read summaries first. Use them to spot calls that look off: a long call with no appointment, a short call from a good lead.
  3. Listen to the flagged ones. Ten minutes of audio teaches more than an hour of reading scores.
  4. Pick one thing to coach. One item per person per week. Say it plainly and give a replacement phrase.
  5. Recheck next week. Did the item improve in new calls?

If you're still building the team, pair this with the CSR training plan so new hires are scored against the same rubric they learned.

Using transcripts for attribution

Call data gets more useful when it's tied to money. A transcript can tell you why a call didn't book, and a CRM record can tell you whether it eventually sold. Together they let you ask which sources produce calls that sound like buyers.

Practical ways to connect them:

  • Record the tracking number or source on each call, and the outcome on the estimate record.
  • Tag calls with a reason code: booked, price shopper, outside area, wrong service, spam.
  • Review the tags monthly by source.

The mechanics of closing that loop are in how to tell which leads became jobs, and the window question (how long after a call a sale still counts) is in the lead attribution window.

An illustrative example, with made-up numbers: you review 40 calls from one source in a month. Transcripts show 12 were outside your service area, 6 were price shoppers asking about a job you don't do, and 22 were real. Of the 22, 11 booked an estimate and 4 signed. Now you know that the source's raw call count overstates what you can use. Of 40 calls, 22 were workable (55%), and 4 became jobs (10% of all calls, about 18% of workable ones). That's a very different picture from "40 calls, 4 jobs." (4 / 40 = 10%, 4 / 22 = 18.2%.)

If a paid source regularly sends calls that don't fit what you ordered, that's evidence worth taking to the provider. Dispute and credit policies differ by provider, and what to look for in a credit policy lays out what should typically qualify.

Limits to keep in mind

Transcription errors. Speech-to-text can mishear names, numbers, and trade terms. Don't copy a transcript's phone number into your CRM without checking it.

Summaries can be wrong in a confident tone. A summary that says "booked for Tuesday" may reflect a tentative remark. Verify against the calendar.

Scores measure behavior, not outcomes. A CSR can tick every box and still lose a call to a bad connection or a homeowner who'd already decided.

Don't punish from the dashboard. Scores are a coaching input. If the first thing a CSR hears is a number, they'll learn to game the number.

Privacy. Store recordings and transcripts with limited access, and delete on a schedule your attorney agrees with.

Calls from AI systems

If you use an AI system to answer calls, score those calls too, against the same rubric plus a few of its own: did it capture the address correctly, did it escalate when it should have. Where to start this week

  2. Make sure calls are captured with a source label.
  3. Build the seven-line scorecard.
  4. Score ten calls by hand, then compare with any tool you try.
  5. Hold one 15-minute coaching session and pick one skill to improve.

Frequently asked questions

What is AI call scoring?

It's software that turns a recorded call into a transcript, then rates it against criteria you set, such as whether the CSR asked for the address or offered an appointment. It saves listening time, but a person should spot-check the scores.

Can I record my customer calls?

Recording rules differ by state and sometimes by who is on the call. This is general information, not legal advice. Talk to an attorney before you record, and decide how you'll tell callers.

How many calls should I review per CSR?

A small, steady sample beats a big occasional pile. Many owners pick a handful per person per week and review them in a short coaching session. Pick a number you'll keep doing.

Can AI summaries replace listening to calls?

Not fully. Summaries can drop or distort details, and they can't hear tone the way you can. Use them to decide which calls to listen to, and listen to the ones that matter.
