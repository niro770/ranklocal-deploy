Home Warranty Work and Contractor Leads: How It Differs From Retail Calls

A home warranty call usually starts with the homeowner calling the warranty company, not you. The company then assigns a contractor from its network and sets the terms. A retail call starts with the homeowner calling you directly, and you set the price. This explainer is part of the what makes homeowners call a contractor guide, and it stays general because contracts differ by company.

What a home warranty is, in plain terms

A home warranty is a service contract a homeowner buys, often during a home purchase or as a renewal, that covers repair or replacement of certain systems and appliances. Coverage, limits, service fees and exclusions vary by plan and provider. This differs from homeowners insurance, which covers different events and works through a different claim process. Homeowners with a plan typically pay an annual or monthly fee plus a service fee when they request a visit.

When something covered breaks, the homeowner contacts the warranty company. The company reviews the request and typically sends it to a contractor in its network. Details of how that works differ by company, so treat what follows as a general picture, not a description of any specific provider.

How the work reaches you

Contractors usually get warranty work by joining a company's contractor network, often through an application and approval step that may include license and insurance checks. After that, the company sends job offers or assignments, and the contractor accepts or declines based on the company's process.

So the "lead" is not a homeowner choosing you. It's a dispatch. The homeowner may not know your name before you arrive, and they didn't compare you with other contractors. That difference drives everything else.

Warranty calls vs retail calls

Here's a general comparison. Terms vary, so check your own agreements.

Factor Warranty-driven work Retail (homeowner calls you)
Who the customer contacts first The warranty company You
Who sets scope Typically the warranty company, within plan coverage You and the homeowner
Who sets price Often a rate or fee schedule set by the company You
Homeowner's choice of contractor Usually limited or none They chose you
Upsell room Depends on the agreement and what's covered Usually more, if the homeowner agrees
Relationship Starts with a dispatch, you earn it on site Starts with a direct call
Volume Can be steady if you accept offers Depends on your marketing

None of these are good or bad in themselves. They change how you run the business.

What contractors usually weigh

Net per job

Compare what you earn per warranty job, after travel time, parts, callbacks and any rules about approvals, with what you'd earn on a retail job of similar size. Do the same for hours, not only dollars. If warranty visits fill otherwise empty slots, that may be worth it. If they displace retail work at a higher rate, it may not.

The calculation uses the same ideas as gross margin and lead cost. The difference is that your "lead cost" is zero or low, while your price is set for you.

Scheduling and service standards

Companies often have expectations for response times, communication and documentation. Read them. Missing them can affect your standing in a network.

Approval and payment process

Some jobs need pre-approval for parts or replacement, which can add time. Payment terms differ. Ask how long approval takes and when you get paid, then plan cash flow around that.

Upselling and customer relationship

On a warranty visit you may see work that isn't covered. How you can discuss and price that work depends on your agreement with the company. Always follow those terms. If you're allowed to offer it, a clear explanation lets the homeowner decide. Repeat retail business can come from a good warranty visit, though it's not guaranteed.

Dependence on one source

If a large share of your revenue comes from a single company, changes to its rates or policies hit you directly. Consider capping warranty work at a share of capacity so you don't depend on it. Pick your own number.

How warranty customers sound on the phone

Contractors who handle both types of work tell the difference in the first thirty seconds. A warranty homeowner often says something like "the warranty company sent you" and may not know the scope or price. A retail caller asks "how much" or "can you come today." Train your office to ask early: "How did you hear about us?" and "Is this going through a warranty company?"

That question belongs in your phone intake script and in your tracking. Label each call by type. It changes how you quote, how you schedule and how you measure results.

Some contractors use warranty work to keep crews busy between retail jobs, and buy calls to grow the higher-margin side. Others skip warranty work entirely. The right mix depends on your price level and your capacity. If you're comparing, calculate cost per job for retail calls and net per job for warranty visits on the same basis, then compare the two.

A practical way to decide

  1. Pull 20 recent jobs of each type if you have them. Include time on site, drive time, parts, callbacks and total revenue.
  2. Compute net per labor hour for each type, not just revenue per job.
  3. Check your capacity. If you're turning away retail jobs, warranty work may crowd them out. If your crews have idle hours, it may fill them.
  4. Read every term. Rates, approvals, exclusions, timelines, termination and anything on exclusivity or non-solicitation. If it's unclear, ask an attorney. This is general information, not legal advice.
  5. Set a cap on warranty share and review it every quarter.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating warranty callers like retail callers, with a retail pitch they didn't ask for.
  • Skipping documentation. Photos and notes protect you if there's a dispute about coverage or workmanship.
  • Ignoring callbacks. A repeat visit on a warranty job may not be paid separately, depending on your agreement, so fix it right the first time.
  • Mixing the numbers. If warranty and retail jobs sit in the same report, you can't see which one is making money. Keep them separate, as the lead attribution idea suggests.
  • Lowering retail prices to match warranty rates. See pricing without racing to the bottom.

The takeaway

Warranty work and retail calls are two different businesses sharing the same trucks. One comes with a set price and a steady flow of dispatches. The other comes with price control and the need to win each customer. Know which you're answering, track them apart and decide on purpose how much of each you want. Frequently asked questions

What is a home warranty call for a contractor?

A homeowner with a service contract reports a covered problem to the warranty company, which then assigns or dispatches a contractor from its network. The contractor works under the warranty company's terms.

How is that different from a retail call?

In a retail call, the homeowner contacts you directly and you set the price. In warranty work, the warranty company typically sets the scope and pay rate, and the homeowner may pay a service fee.

Is warranty work a good fit for every contractor?

It depends on your pricing, overhead and goals. Terms vary by company, so read the agreement and compare the net per job with your retail work.

Can I get retail leads and do warranty work together?

Many contractors do both, using warranty work to fill gaps and retail calls for higher-margin jobs. Track each separately so you can see which one pays.
