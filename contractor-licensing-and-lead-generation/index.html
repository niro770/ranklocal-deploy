Contractor Licensing and Lead Generation: Why a License Check Belongs at Intake

A license check belongs in lead intake because it keeps you from paying for, quoting and scheduling work you may not be allowed to do. It also keeps your lead vendor honest about the trades and areas it sells. Licensing rules differ by state and by trade, so this page stays general. Check your state licensing board for the rules that apply to you.

This is general information, not legal advice. For anything that affects whether you can lawfully do a job, ask your licensing board and an attorney. The wider quality picture is in the call quality and compliance guide.

The basic problem

A homeowner calls and says, "I need my whole electrical panel replaced." You're a licensed handyman. Or you're a licensed HVAC contractor and the caller wants a roof. Or the job's value is above what your license allows. Or the property is in a county that requires a separate local registration you don't have.

Each of those calls cost you money, tied up an estimator and could lead to a bad situation if someone quotes it anyway. The cost isn't only the lead. It's the drive, the estimate and the risk of promising work you can't legally perform.

Intake is the cheapest place to catch it. A thirty-second question at the start of the call costs far less than a wasted site visit.

Why licensing varies and what that means for you

There's no single national contractor license for home services. Requirements are set at the state level, and some are set by cities or counties. In general terms, things that can vary include:

  • Which trades need a license at all.
  • Whether there are classifications or license types within a trade.
  • Whether limits apply by job value.
  • Whether you need a separate registration for certain work, such as work involving specific materials or regulated systems.
  • Whether the business, the qualifying individual or both must be licensed.
  • Bonding and insurance requirements.

Don't assume a rule you learned in one state applies in another, or that a rule that applied to one trade applies to the next. This page deliberately makes no claims about any particular state. Your state's licensing board is the authority, and its website usually has a license lookup and a plain-language description of who needs what.

Where a license check fits in lead intake

1. Before you buy: scope the lead source

When you evaluate a call source, ask what trades and service areas it actually covers and whether it filters for your specific license scope. If you're a roofer, calls for gutters or siding may or may not fit your license and your business. The questions to ask a lead generation company include the fit questions to put to any vendor.

A vendor also has a responsibility to describe truthfully what it sells. A source that routes calls to unlicensed operators, or sells you as licensed for things you aren't, creates risk for everyone involved. Check with an attorney if a vendor's marketing language says anything about your license or credentials.

2. At the phone: confirm the job fits your license

Your intake script should include a quick scope check. It doesn't need to sound legal. Examples:

  • "Just to confirm, you're looking for a full replacement, not a repair?"
  • "Is this a single-family home? Where's the property?"
  • "Is the work inside or outside the house?"
  • "Roughly what size project are you picturing?"

These line up with the questions in how to qualify a home service caller and the contractor phone intake script. Add one or two that map to the edges of your license: property type, job size, location and work type.

3. Before the estimate: check the location and rules

Service areas matter. If your license or registration covers certain jurisdictions, confirm the address before scheduling. A caller 40 miles outside your area, or across a state line, may need a different license or may be outside what you can lawfully do. Treat location verification as part of intake rather than something to sort out at the door.

4. On the homeowner's side: show your license

Callers increasingly check credentials. Put your license number on your website, trucks, estimates and listings, and say it on the phone when asked. That helps with fake listing and imitation problems too, because it gives homeowners a verifiable way to separate you from a lookalike.

What a license check at intake can look like

A short internal checklist for your CSR or office staff:

  1. Does the requested work match a trade we're licensed to perform?
  2. Is the property in a jurisdiction where we're licensed and registered to work?
  3. Does the job size fall within any limits on our license type?
  4. Are there special requirements for this kind of work, such as permits, certifications or inspections?
  5. If the answer to any of these is uncertain, who in the company decides, and is the estimator told before the visit?

Keep it short. A long script kills conversion, and the point is to filter out clear mismatches, not to interrogate callers. If a call fails the check, have a polite redirect ready: "We don't do that type of work, but I can suggest you check your state's license lookup for a licensed contractor in that trade."

Unlicensed work: why it's a risk for everyone

Without describing any particular state's rules, there are general reasons to avoid work outside your license:

  • Enforcement risk. Licensing boards and local authorities can impose penalties for unlicensed work, and the penalties vary by place.
  • Contract risk. In some places, contracts for work done without a required license can be hard to enforce. Ask an attorney whether this applies to you.
  • Insurance risk. Coverage may depend on proper licensing. Check your policy and talk to your agent.
  • Reputation risk. A homeowner who finds out you weren't licensed for the job will say so in reviews.

These are reasons to check, not legal conclusions. Your attorney can tell you how they apply where you work.

Licensing and lead sources

Two things connect licensing to the lead side of the business.

Vendor screening. Some lead sources verify contractor licenses before sending leads, others don't. Ask. Marketplaces and call sources differ widely, and what a vendor says about "verified" or "screened" contractors should be defined in writing. See the 15-point vendor audit. For Google Local Services Ads, Google runs verification badge programs; check Google's current help pages for what each involves and requires.

Homeowner expectations. Callers who find you through a paid source often assume you're vetted. If you aren't properly licensed, that assumption doesn't protect you. If you are, it's a selling point you can state out loud on the call.

Trade examples, kept general

The details differ, but the shape of the check repeats across trades:

  • Roofing and exterior work. Scope often covers repair, replacement and sometimes related exterior trades. See roofing leads and fence leads. Ask the board what's covered.
  • Landscaping and pest control. Some work, such as applying certain products, can be regulated differently from general landscaping or general service. See landscaping leads and pest control leads. Check your state agency.
  • Garage door work. Garage door repair may involve electrical or safety considerations that matter for scope. See garage door repair leads.
  • Plumbing, HVAC and electrical. These are often licensed trades with their own rules. Your board will say.

Don't read these as claims about any state. They're prompts for questions to ask your board.

How to keep the habit

  • Put your license number in the intake script's opening for calls you run through a CSR.
  • Review recordings now and then and listen for whether the scope question got asked. A call-scoring scorecard can include it.
  • Revisit your license scope once a year and when you add a service line, so the script stays accurate.
  • Track calls rejected for scope. If a vendor sends many of them, take it to the vendor and use the dispute process if the terms allow.

Frequently asked questions

Do all contractors need a license to take leads?

Licensing rules differ by state, trade and sometimes by city or county, and by job size. Check your state licensing board and local rules, and ask an attorney if you're unsure.

Why does licensing matter for lead generation?

Leads for work you can't legally perform, or in a place or for a trade you aren't licensed for, waste money and can create legal risk. A check at intake keeps those calls out of your pipeline.

Where do I verify a contractor license?

Your state's contractor or trade licensing board is the authoritative place, and many publish a lookup on their website. Local jurisdictions may have their own requirements.

Is this legal advice?

No. It's general information, and you should check with your state licensing board and an attorney about your situation.
