How to Use Google Reviews to Lift Your Close Rate

Reviews lift your close rate by answering the question a homeowner has before they book: can I trust this company? Make them easy to find, ask for one after every finished job, reply to each one, and mention them on the phone. It's one of the cheaper levers in growing a home-service company.

Why reviews matter after the phone rings

Most contractor marketing talks about getting the call. Reviews work on the next step. A homeowner who calls three companies about a failing water heater, or a roofer after a storm, often checks each one's profile before agreeing to a visit. Your profile is the second thing they see after your phone voice.

That's most useful when you buy inbound calls. Every call costs something. If the homeowner then looks you up and finds six reviews from three years ago, you've paid to lose them at the last step. With exclusive calls, the caller reached you alone, but they can still decide you aren't the one to send.

I won't hand you a percentage for how much reviews lift conversion, because the figures floating around are rarely sourced and vary by trade. Measure it yourself, as described below.

What homeowners look for

When they open your profile, they're scanning for a few things:

  • Recency. Reviews from this month say you're active. A gap of a year raises a question.
  • Volume relative to nearby competitors. They compare, even if they won't say so.
  • The rating, and the bad ones. One angry review among many good ones reads as normal. A pattern of the same complaint doesn't.
  • Specifics. "Replaced our AC on a Sunday in July" beats "great service."
  • How you reply. A calm owner reply to a complaint can win more trust than a five-star review.
  • Photos. Real job photos show you actually do the work.

Step 1: Fix the profile before asking for more

Do a quick check of your Google Business Profile as a homeowner would see it. Are your hours, service area, categories and phone number right? Do you have recent job photos? Does the first review a visitor sees say something useful? For the profile itself, see Google's own help pages, since the features change.

Step 2: Ask every customer, the same way

Reviews come from a process, not from hoping. The pattern that works for most shops:

  1. Pick the moment. Right after the job is finished and the customer has confirmed they're happy, or on the day payment is collected.
  2. Pick the person. The tech or installer who did the work asks in person: "If you're happy with it, we'd appreciate a review." Then the office follows with a text.
  3. Send a direct link. Every extra step loses people. Use the link Google gives you for your profile's review box and put it in a text message. Make sure it opens correctly on a phone.
  4. Keep the message short. Name, thanks, link. Something like: "Hi Dana, thanks for choosing us for your furnace install. If you have a minute, a Google review helps a lot: [link]."
  5. Follow up once. One reminder a few days later, then stop.
  6. Track it. Count requests sent and reviews received per week. If you ask ten people and get one, that's your starting rate.

Don't wait for a big batch. A steady trickle looks natural and keeps recency high.

What not to do

  • Don't pay or discount for reviews. Google's policies restrict incentivized reviews. Read the current rules in Google's help pages before you build your process.
  • Don't screen first. Asking "were you happy?" and only sending happy customers the link is called review gating, and Google's policy discourages it. Check the current wording.
  • Don't write your own or ask friends who weren't customers. It breaks the rules and homeowners can often tell.
  • Don't mass-text old customers all at once. Spread requests out.
  • Don't ask unhappy customers to remove reviews as a trade. Fix the problem, then let them decide.

Step 3: Reply to every review

Replies are public and permanent, and the audience is the next homeowner.

Replying to good reviews

Keep it short, human and specific. Use the customer's first name, mention the job, and thank them.

Thanks, Dana. A furnace swap in January is nobody's idea of fun, so we're glad the crew got you warm by dinner. Call us any time.

Avoid copy-paste replies. Three identical responses in a row look automated.

Replying to bad reviews

  1. Wait an hour. Then reply the same day or next.
  2. Thank them, don't argue. "Thanks for telling us."
  3. Own what's true. If you were late, say so.
  4. State the facts only if necessary, briefly and without blame.
  5. Move it offline. Give a name and a direct phone number.
  6. Don't share private details about the customer or job.

Example:

We're sorry the install ran past the day we promised, Mr. Lopez. That's on us, and it isn't how we want customers to feel. Please call our office manager, Tess, at the number on our profile so we can make it right.

If a review looks fake or breaks Google's rules, you can report it through Google. Do that quietly, and still reply if it stays up.

Step 4: Use reviews on the call

Reviews help close when you mention them at the right moment. They don't help if nobody sees them.

  • On the phone. When a caller says they're comparing, say "You'll see our recent reviews on Google, and you can check them while we're talking." A confident caller gets the same reassurance a price shopper wants. See how to handle price shoppers.
  • In the confirmation text. Send the appointment confirmation with your company name, your tech's name and a link to your profile.
  • In the quote. A line pointing to your most relevant review, such as the same job type in the same neighborhood, supports your price. This helps with pricing without racing to the bottom.
  • In your intake script. Add one line about reviews to your phone intake script.

Step 5: Measure whether it moves your close rate

You can test this without a dashboard.

  1. Ask every new caller how they found you and what made them call. Write the answer in the call log.
  2. Count mentions. How many say "your reviews" or "I read about you"?
  3. Track call-to-estimate and estimate-to-job rate by month. See call-to-estimate rate.
  4. Compare periods. If the rate climbs as review count and recency climb, you have a signal. If nothing changes, look at the phone call and the quote.
  5. Check the near misses. When a booked caller cancels, ask what they saw.

Many things change at once, such as season and weather, so treat this as directional.

Reviews by trade

The content of a strong review differs.

  • Roofing and fence: Reviews that mention cleanup, communication and finishing on time matter because the job is big and visible.
  • HVAC and plumbing: Response time and honesty about whether a repair or replacement was needed are what homeowners worry about.
  • Pest, lawn and recurring services: Reviews about consistency over months matter more than one visit.
  • Emergency work: "Showed up fast, fair price" is the sentence people scan for.

Give your techs a script that encourages specifics: "If you mention what we fixed, it helps the next person."

Common mistakes

  • Asking once a year. Reviews fade quickly. Build the habit into the job close-out.
  • Ignoring complaints. A reply a month later looks worse than none.
  • Leaving the link to the customer to find. Send it.
  • Buying more calls before fixing the profile. More calls into a weak profile means a higher cost per job. The capacity and conversion check is worth running first.

Frequently asked questions

Do Google reviews affect close rate?

Homeowners often check reviews between the call and the booking, so a thin or poor profile can lose you a job you already paid for. You can test it yourself by asking new callers how they found you and what made them choose you.

When is the best time to ask for a review?

Right after the job, while the homeowner is happy and the work is fresh. Ask by text with a direct link, from the person who did the work or the office within a day or two.

Can I offer a discount for a review?

Google's policies restrict incentivized reviews and review gating, so don't offer rewards. Check Google's current review policy before you build a process.

How should I reply to a bad review?

Reply promptly and calmly, thank them, own what you can, and offer to fix it offline. Write for the next homeowner reading it, not for the reviewer.
