What to Do With Missed Calls: Callback Timing, Text-Back, and Voicemail Wording

When you miss a lead call, text the caller within a minute or two, call back within about 15 minutes if it's business hours, and make your voicemail greeting give them a reason to leave a message. Then log the miss and keep trying on a schedule. Below are timing rules and wording you can copy.

Missed calls happen to every shop. The goal isn't zero, it's a process so a miss turns into a conversation rather than a lost job. This is part of the larger flow in how to answer contractor leads, and the reasons timing matters are in speed to lead for contractors.

Triage first: not all missed calls are equal

Sort the miss before you react.

Type What it looks like Priority
New caller, left a message Unknown number, voicemail with a job Highest
New caller, no message Unknown number, short or no voicemail High
Existing customer Known name or number High, but handled differently
Vendor or solicitor Sales pitch, robocall pattern Low
Wrong number Short call, no message Ignore

If you buy calls, new callers from your lead source are the top priority because you've paid for them. Under pay-per-call, a qualifying call is one over 60 seconds at RankLocal, so a short unanswered ring may not even be billable. Still, check your call log for anything from a lead source.

The callback timeline

Here's a schedule you can actually follow. It assumes a missed call during business hours.

When Action
Within 1 to 2 minutes Automatic or manual text
Within 15 minutes First callback attempt
2 to 3 hours later Second callback attempt if no answer
Same evening Short text if still no contact
Next morning Third attempt, early
Day 3 One more call plus text, then move to the follow-up sequence

These timings are suggestions based on how people behave, not benchmarks. If you can only manage a 30-minute callback, hit it every time rather than promising 15 and missing it.

For calls that come in after hours, see the after-hours guide. The plan there decides whether the first response is a person, a recording, or a text.

Text-back wording that works

A good text-back is short, sounds human, and tells the caller what happens next. It also asks a question that's easy to answer.

Right after a missed call:

"Hi, this is [Name] with [Company]. Sorry we missed your call. I'm [on a job / with another customer] and will call you back in about 15 minutes. What's going on, so I can be ready?"

If they replied with details:

"Thanks, [First Name]. Got it. I'll call you at [time] to get you scheduled. Is this still the best number?"

If you can't reach them after two tries:

"Hi [First Name], [Name] from [Company] again. I tried you a couple of times about your [leak / AC / fence]. Want me to text you a couple of times we could come out? Just reply with the one that works."

When the issue sounds urgent:

"[Name] from [Company]. Saw you called about [issue]. If it's an emergency, call me directly at [cell] and I'll pick up."

Keep each text under about 320 characters, avoid links in the first message, and don't use all caps. Don't send marketing blasts through this channel.

Texting rules

Texting prospects touches consumer-protection and telemarketing rules, and the details vary by situation. A reply to a call someone just made is different from a promotional message. This is general information, not legal advice. Read TCPA lead compliance and ask an attorney about your own setup, especially if you automate messages.

Voicemail greeting wording

Your voicemail is a small sales page. Most owners record one in 2014 and never touch it. Here are better ones.

Business hours, standard:

"You've reached [Company]. We're on a job right now, but we call everyone back within the hour. Leave your name, number, and what's going on, and we'll call you right back. If it's an emergency, press 1 and we'll get you to a person."

After hours:

"You've reached [Company]. Our office is closed, but we check messages first thing at [time]. If you have an emergency like a leak, no heat, or storm damage, call [emergency number]. Otherwise leave your name, number, and address, and we'll call you first thing."

Busy season:

"Thanks for calling [Company]. It's a busy week, so we may take a little longer than usual to return calls, but we do call everyone back. Leave your name and number and a good time to reach you."

What to include

  • The company name, so they know they reached the right place.
  • A time frame you actually meet.
  • What to say: name, number, and the problem.
  • An emergency option, if you offer one.

What to cut

  • "Your call is very important to us."
  • Long menus.
  • Jokes that wear thin.
  • Personal cell numbers if you don't want them on the internet. Use a dedicated line.

Test it by calling your own number and listening as a stranger would. If it takes more than 20 seconds to get to the beep, shorten it.

When they don't pick up the callback

Many people don't answer calls from unknown numbers. That's why the text matters. After a missed callback:

  1. Leave a short voicemail: "Hi [First Name], it's [Name] at [Company] returning your call about [issue]. I'll try you again at [time], or you can reach me at [number]."
  2. Send the text version.
  3. Log the attempt with the time.

Don't leave five voicemails in a day. That reads as desperation. Two calls, one text, one voicemail is plenty for day one.

Build a missed-call log

You can't fix what you don't count. Keep a simple sheet or CRM list.

Column Why
Date and time Shows when you miss the most
Number Needed for callbacks
Source Ties to where the lead came from
First response time Your actual speed
Outcome Booked, no answer, not a fit

At the end of the month, add up how many misses became booked estimates and how long the first response took. Patterns appear fast. Maybe you miss most calls between noon and 1 p.m., or on Mondays when crews head out. Then you can schedule coverage for those windows. If you record calls, see call tracking for contractors, and ask an attorney about recording laws in your state.

An illustrative example

Say you miss 12 calls in a week, a hypothetical figure. You text all 12 within two minutes. You call back 12 within 20 minutes. Let's say 7 pick up, 3 reply to the text, and 2 never respond. Of the 10 you reach, 4 book estimates. That's 4 estimates from 12 missed calls, a rate of one in three.

Without the process, suppose you'd waited until evening to call back. You might have reached 5, booked 1 or 2. The numbers are made up, but the gap is the point: fast, layered contact turns misses into bookings at a higher rate than a single slow callback.

Reduce misses at the source

Callbacks are the cleanup. Fewer misses is the cure.

  • Forward calls to a cell during job hours.
  • Share the phone among two people on a ring group.
  • Block off an hour at midday for returning calls.
  • Use an answering option for peak periods, compared in answering service vs in-house CSR.
  • Train the person who picks up, covered in how to train a contractor CSR.

When someone does call back and book, use the intake script so the first real conversation goes the same way every time.

Frequently asked questions

How soon should I call back a missed lead?

As soon as you can, and within about 15 minutes during business hours if you can manage it. Send a text within a minute or two to hold the lead while you get to a phone.

Should I call or text a missed caller?

Do both, in that order of speed. A text goes out immediately, then you call. Many people ignore unknown numbers but read a text, so the text makes the callback more likely to be answered.

How many times should I try a missed caller?

Two calls on the first day, spaced a few hours apart, plus the text, then a follow-up the next day. After that, switch to a slower follow-up sequence and stop calling repeatedly.

Does an automatic text-back count as a legitimate response?

It buys time, but it doesn't replace a human. Keep texts relevant to the call they just made, and ask an attorney about consent and texting rules for your state and situation.
