How to Score Contractor Sales Calls: A Scorecard and What to Listen For

Score contractor sales calls with a simple 10-point scorecard: greeting, information captured, qualifying questions, listening, handling price, offering a booking, setting expectations and closing the call. Review five to ten recordings per person weekly, score them against the same sheet, and coach on the one or two habits that cost you the most bookings.

This template belongs to the guide to answering contractor leads. It works for office staff, owners who answer their own phones, and answering services you want to hold to a standard.

Why score calls at all

If you only look at outcomes (booked or not), you can't tell why. A call might fail because the lead was poor, the caller was in a hurry, or your CSR skipped the booking question. Scoring separates what the caller brought from what your team did, so you coach the right thing.

It also helps you evaluate lead sources. If a source produces calls where your team scores well but few book, the problem is likely the lead. If your team scores poorly on every source, the problem is the phone process. Call tracking tells you where a call came from, and scoring tells you what happened on it.

Recording consent: check before you record

Laws about recording phone calls vary by state, and some places require consent from everyone on the call. This is general information, not legal advice. Talk to an attorney about what applies to your business, to callers in other states, and to how to give notice, such as a short message at the start of the call. Set that up first, then record. If you don't record, you can still score live by listening in where permitted or by reviewing call notes, but recordings are far better for coaching.

The scorecard

Score each line 0, 1 or 2. Zero means missed, one means partial, two means done well. The maximum is 20. Print it or copy it into a spreadsheet.

# Category What a 2 sounds like Score (0-2)
1 Answer and greeting Answered quickly, company name, own name, friendly tone
2 Contact details captured Name, callback number and address, early in the call
3 Listening Let the caller describe the problem, didn't interrupt, repeated key facts
4 Scope questions Asked specific questions that fit the trade
5 Decision-maker and timeline Asked who approves, when they need it, and why
6 Price handling Gave a range or factors, kept control of the call
7 Booking offer Asked for the appointment with two specific times
8 Expectations Explained what happens next, who comes, how long
9 Read-back and close Repeated date, time, address, and ended warmly
10 Tone and professionalism Calm, confident, no jargon, no rushing

Reading the total

  • 16 to 20: Strong call. Note what worked and share it.
  • 11 to 15: Decent, with one or two gaps worth coaching.
  • 6 to 10: The process is breaking down. Practice and re-score.
  • 0 to 5: Review immediately and check whether training happened at all.

Treat the bands as guides, not grades you hand out as punishment. The goal is a conversation about patterns.

Add two flags outside the score

Some details don't belong in the 20 points but are worth tagging:

  • Outcome: booked, callback set, no-fit, lost, spam.
  • Call type: new lead, existing customer, vendor, wrong number.

Keeping outcome apart from score avoids a trap: rewarding a CSR for easy calls and penalizing them for hard ones.

What to listen for in a recording

Scoring is only as good as the listening. Here's what to pay attention to, in the order calls unfold.

The first ten seconds

  • How many rings before pickup? Long rings lose people.
  • Is the greeting clear? "Hello?" sounds like a personal phone. Callers hesitate.
  • Is there background noise, such as a loud shop or a barking dog? It may be fine, but listen to how the caller reacts.

The middle of the call

  • Talk ratio. In a good intake call the caller does most of the talking early, then the CSR leads. If the CSR talks for two minutes before the caller says a word, that's a problem.
  • Dead air. Long silences while someone types or hunts for a calendar tell the caller you're disorganized.
  • Interruptions. Count them.
  • Repeats. If the CSR asks the same question twice, the caller will notice.
  • Jargon. "Do you need a condenser or an air handler?" assumes knowledge a homeowner may not have.

Price and objections

  • Does the CSR flinch or dodge when price comes up?
  • Do they offer a defensible range and tie it to the visit?
  • Do they stay polite when the caller pushes?

See how to handle price shoppers on the phone for the lines to compare against.

The close

This is where most calls leak. Listen for whether the CSR actually asked for the appointment, offered specific times, and read back the details. A call that ends with "Okay, give us a call if you want to move forward" has no close. The qualifying steps lead up to it.

After the call

Check the notes. Do they match the recording? Missing address or vague scope notes mean the estimator arrives unprepared.

How to sample calls

You don't have time to review everything, and you shouldn't. Pull a mix.

  • Booked calls: to learn what's working.
  • Calls that ended without a booking: to learn what didn't.
  • Short calls: under 30 seconds can be hang-ups, wrong numbers or abrupt starts.
  • Long calls with no booking: often a price conversation that stalled.
  • Missed-call callbacks: to check speed and wording.

A simple weekly rhythm for a small shop:

  1. Pull five to ten calls per person.
  2. Score them in one 45-minute block.
  3. Pick the single most common gap.
  4. Share one short example with the team.
  5. Practice that one skill for the next week.

Resist the urge to fix everything at once. Teams improve faster when they work on one habit at a time.

Turning scores into coaching

Numbers alone don't change behavior. Pair each review with a short conversation.

A coaching format that works: 1. Ask the CSR what they think went well. People usually spot their own gaps. 2. Play a thirty-second clip, not the full call. 3. Say what you heard, in specific terms: "You asked for the address after four minutes. Let's move it to the first minute." 4. Rehearse the better version together, out loud. 5. Agree on one thing to try this week.

Praise what's strong and be specific about it. "Great job" teaches nothing. "I liked how you repeated the leak location back to her" teaches something.

Group scores by pattern, not person

Over a month, tally the lowest-scoring category across the team. If most people miss the booking offer, the fix is a script or a standing reminder. If one person misses everything, that's a training and fit conversation. The CSR training guide covers onboarding and practice plans.

Connect scores to results

Scoring becomes more valuable when you line it up with outcomes over time. Track, per CSR and per source:

  • Average score
  • Booked estimates divided by qualifying calls, which is your call-to-estimate rate
  • No-show rate
  • Time to first callback on missed calls

If the score rises and the booking rate doesn't, check whether your scorecard rewards the right behavior. You may be scoring politeness more heavily than closing. Adjust the weights.

Scoring answering services and outsourced staff

If an answering service takes your calls, you can use the same scorecard. Ask for recordings, score a sample, and send specific feedback. Agree on the standards in writing before you start. If you're comparing in-house and outsourced options, the scorecard gives you a like-for-like measure.

Common mistakes

  • Scoring only lost calls. You'll miss the habits that win.
  • Using a 50-point form. Nobody finishes it. Ten lines is enough.
  • Changing the scorecard every week. You can't compare scores over time.
  • Using scores to punish. People hide mistakes when scores feel dangerous.
  • Never sharing good calls. Play the best call at a team meeting.
  • Skipping the notes check. A great call with bad notes still produces a bad estimate visit.

Starter plan for week one

  1. Confirm your recording and notice approach with an attorney.
  2. Copy the scorecard into a sheet.
  3. Pull five calls per person.
  4. Score them together once, so everyone calibrates.
  5. Choose one habit to coach.
  6. Review again in a week and compare.

Frequently asked questions

How many calls should I review each week?

Five to ten calls per person per week is a workable sample for a small shop. Include a mix of booked, lost and short calls so you don't only hear the good ones.

What should a contractor call scorecard measure?

Greeting, information capture, qualification, handling objections, offering a booking and closing the call with a clear next step. Score what the caller experienced and what your team did, not whether the job closed.

Can I record calls without telling callers?

Recording laws vary by state, and some require consent from everyone on the call. Consult an attorney about what applies to your business and where your callers are located.

Should I score calls with software or by hand?

Start by hand with a simple sheet so you learn what matters. Software can help once you have a stable scorecard and enough call volume to justify it.
