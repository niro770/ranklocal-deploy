Winter Lead Strategy for Roofers, Fence and Landscaping Contractors

Winter doesn't have to be a dead zone for exterior trades. Use it to sell and schedule spring work, answer the repair calls that cold weather creates, and fix the office habits you ignored in peak season. This page expands on the seasonal triggers in what makes homeowners call a contractor and focuses on what to do when production slows.

What winter demand looks like

Winter varies a lot by region. A roofer in Minnesota and a roofer in Florida have different problems. Here's the general pattern in cold-weather markets, described in broad strokes rather than with national numbers:

  • Roofing: fewer full replacements, more leak and ice-damage repairs, storm and wind damage, and estimates for homeowners planning ahead.
  • Fence: installs slow when the ground freezes, but repair calls after wind and snow still come in, and homeowners start asking about spring pricing.
  • Landscaping: planting stops, though snow removal, planning, design work and spring contract signups can continue.

Check your own call logs and job history for the last two winters. If you've tracked by month, you already have the answer for your market. The seasonal lead demand by trade reference gives a qualitative month-by-month view.

Step 1: Sort your winter work into three buckets

Not all winter calls are equal. Sort them as they arrive.

Bucket Examples What to do
Urgent now Active roof leak, fallen fence section, storm damage Respond fast, tarp or secure, then schedule repair
Book now, build later Roof replacement, new fence, patio or landscape project Estimate now, sign, and set a spring start date
Planning only Early price checks, "thinking about it" Follow up on a schedule, don't let them go cold

The first bucket is where you get paid in January. The second is where you fill March and April. The third is a pipeline you have to work deliberately, because these homeowners will talk to several companies and forget most of them.

Step 2: Sell spring work in winter

Many homeowners will accept a spring start date if you make it easy. A few practices help:

  • Offer a calendar slot. "I can put you on the schedule for the second week of April" is concrete. "Call us in spring" is not.
  • Take a modest deposit or a signed agreement, with clear terms about what happens if weather or materials delay the start. Follow your state's rules on deposits and contract terms.
  • Order materials early. For fence and roofing, supply timing can matter in busy months. Confirm what you can reserve.
  • Offer winter pricing only if the math works. A discount that fills the calendar but eats your margin is a bad trade. See gross margin and lead cost before you discount.

Estimating in winter also has a quiet advantage. Your estimator isn't rushing between six appointments a day, so quotes can be more careful and follow-up more consistent.

Step 3: Protect cash flow before the slow months

The hardest part of winter is often cash, not leads. A few habits help:

  1. Build a reserve in peak season. Set aside a fixed share of each completed job for the slow months. Decide the share in advance.
  2. Invoice promptly and collect on schedule. Slow-paying jobs hurt more when there's less new revenue coming in. Tracking every job from call to payment starts with knowing which leads became jobs.
  3. Review recurring costs. Look at software, vehicles, ads and subscriptions. Keep what you can tie to booked jobs.
  4. Plan a seasonal staffing agreement. Talk to crew members about winter hours well before you need to.

A marketing budget built as a percentage of revenue will shrink in winter automatically. Decide whether you want that. Some contractors keep marketing steady to hold their place while others are quiet, and others pull back. Neither is right in general. Measure what you get.

Step 4: Decide how to buy calls in the off-season

If you buy leads, winter is a good time to look at the structure of what you pay for.

  • Fixed monthly spends (retainers, ad budgets, memberships) keep charging when the phone is quiet. Check how much of that spend produced booked jobs last winter.
  • Pay per qualifying call means you pay when a homeowner actually calls and stays on the line. With pay-per-call, a quiet month costs less because there are fewer billable calls, and a busy week costs more because there are more. The billable call definition explains the 60-second threshold.
  • Shared leads that go to several contractors can get harder when demand is thin, because several contractors may be chasing fewer inquiries. See exclusive vs shared leads.

Step 5: Fix the office while it's quiet

Winter is the cheapest time to work on the phones and processes that decide close rates in spring. Candidate projects:

  • Write or tighten your intake script. The phone intake script template gives you a starting point.
  • Listen to 20 recorded calls from the fall and note where callers dropped off. Use the call scoring scorecard to keep it consistent.
  • Set up call tracking so you can see which sources produced jobs. See call tracking for contractors.
  • Train your CSR with the onboarding plan so a new hire is ready by March.
  • Ask for reviews from your fall customers. Reviews help in spring when homeowners compare you to others.
  • Clean up your customer list. Past customers are the cheapest source of repeat and referral work.

Trade-specific ideas

Roofing

Keep a crew available for emergency repairs and tarping. Promote leak inspections and ice-damage assessments if relevant in your area. Estimate replacements all winter and book them for the first dry weeks. If a major storm hits, shift to the plan in storm season lead strategy.

Fence

Talk to homeowners about permits, property lines and HOA approvals now, since those slow spring starts. Offer wind-damage repairs. Build a spring waitlist and confirm material orders with your suppliers.

Landscaping

Sell design work, contracts for lawn and bed maintenance, and spring cleanups. If you offer snow removal, treat it as a separate service with its own pricing and capacity. Recurring work smooths revenue. See recurring revenue trades and lead value for how the lifetime value of a recurring customer changes what you can pay for a call.

A simple winter checklist

  • Know what share of your winter calls are urgent, plan-ahead and price-check.
  • Answer every call and follow up within a day.
  • Book spring start dates with clear written terms.
  • Set a spending limit by source and review it monthly against booked jobs.
  • Use quiet hours for training, scripts and tracking.

Frequently asked questions

Do homeowners still call exterior contractors in winter?

Yes, but for different reasons. Calls shift toward leak repairs, storm damage, estimates for spring projects and planning ahead, depending on your climate.

Should I cut marketing spend in the off-season?

Not automatically. Spend should follow your capacity and the quality of the calls, and some trades book spring work in winter. Cut what doesn't produce jobs, and keep what does.

What can fence and landscaping companies sell in winter?

Estimates and scheduled installs for spring, plus design, permits and materials ordering. Many homeowners prefer to lock in a spot on the calendar before the busy season.

How do I keep my crew employed in winter?

Use the slow weeks for equipment maintenance, shop work and training, add services you're licensed for that aren't weather-limited, or agree on a seasonal schedule with crew members ahead of time.
