Estimate Appointment No-Shows for Contractors: Why They Happen and How to Cut Them

Homeowners skip estimate appointments for four main reasons: they forgot, they found a faster quote, they hit a conflict and couldn't easily reschedule, or they were never fully committed. A confirmation, a day-before reminder, a morning-of text and an on-the-way message fixes most of the first and third. The rest comes from how you book.

This is one piece of the broader guide to answering contractor leads, and it matters because a no-show is the most expensive miss after a missed call. You already paid to get the phone to ring, answered it and booked the visit. Then a truck and an estimator drove out for nothing.

What a no-show actually costs

You don't need industry statistics to see the damage. Here's an illustrative example with made-up numbers:

  • An estimator's loaded cost is $45 an hour, including vehicle.
  • A typical estimate visit, including drive time both ways, takes 90 minutes.
  • A no-show wastes that time at $45 x 1.5 = $67.50, plus the slot you could have given to someone else.

Say your shop books 40 estimates a month and 6 don't show. That's 6 x $67.50 = $405 of direct cost, plus six lost appointment slots. If your average job nets $2,000 and your close rate on completed estimates is 25 percent, those six slots would have produced 6 x 25% = 1.5 jobs, or about $3,000 in revenue. The visible cost is $405. The invisible cost is larger.

Run your own numbers. The point is that no-shows are a cost you can reduce with fairly cheap fixes.

Why no-shows happen

1. They forgot

The booking happened during a stressful moment: a leak, a heat wave, a roof that finally gave out. Days later, the memory has faded. No reminder, no show.

2. They got a faster answer elsewhere

The homeowner called three companies. Whoever showed up first, or sent a clear number first, got the job. Your appointment, set three days out, lost to someone who came this afternoon. This is why speed to lead feeds directly into attendance. The longer the gap between call and visit, the more chances for a competitor to step in.

3. They couldn't reschedule easily

A homeowner who has a conflict and can't find a simple way to move the visit often just doesn't answer the door. If the only option is calling during office hours, many won't bother.

4. They weren't really committed

They were price shopping and said yes to get you off the phone. See how to handle price shoppers for what to do before you book those.

5. The wrong person was booked

The caller couldn't make the decision, or the decision-maker wasn't at the appointment. The visit might happen, but the estimate goes nowhere. Qualifying the caller on ownership and who approves the work prevents this.

6. Confusion about the visit itself

Unclear arrival windows, a wrong address in the system, no idea who is coming or what to expect. Vague bookings produce vague attendance.

Fix the booking before you fix the reminders

Reminders help, but a weak booking has to be fixed upstream. Four habits make appointments stick:

  • Offer two specific times. "Tuesday at 10 or Wednesday at 2?" gets a commitment. "When works for you?" gets a shrug.
  • Book as soon as is practical. For non-emergencies, same-day or next-day beats next week.
  • Confirm who has to be there. If a spouse or landlord decides, schedule when they can attend.
  • Explain what happens. "Marcus will arrive between 10 and 11, walk the property for about 30 minutes, and send a written estimate within two days."
  • Read back the details. Name, address, date, window and phone number.

A reminder cadence that works

Use text and, where it fits, a call. Keep every message short, with the company name, the time, the address and a way to reschedule.

Touch When Channel Goal
Confirmation Immediately after booking Text, plus email if you have one Lock in the details
Day-before reminder Late morning or afternoon the day before Text Prompt a reply: confirm or reschedule
Morning-of reminder 2 to 3 hours before the window Text Last chance to cancel cleanly
On-the-way When the estimator leaves Text, with name and photo if you can Make the arrival expected
High-value call Day before, for big jobs Phone Personal check-in

For appointments booked more than three days out, add a reminder at the midpoint, so there are never more than two days of silence.

Sample messages

Confirmation: "Hi Dana, this is [Company]. You're booked for a free estimate Tuesday, May 14, between 10 and 11 AM at 123 Oak St. Marcus will be your estimator. Reply C to confirm or R to reschedule."

Day before: "Reminder from [Company]: Marcus is scheduled at your home tomorrow between 10 and 11 AM. Will you or another decision-maker be there? Reply Y to confirm or R to move it."

Morning of: "Good morning, Dana. Marcus will arrive between 10 and 11 today. If anything changed, reply or call [number] and we'll rebook right away."

On the way: "Marcus from [Company] is on the way and should arrive in about 20 minutes. His number is [number]."

After no reply to the day-before text: Call. Many people read texts and never answer. A thirty-second call the evening before can save a visit.

Make rescheduling easy

Make the reschedule path one reply or one click. Homeowners who cancel on their own terms are better for you than ghosts, because you can reuse the slot and re-engage the person. Add a line such as "Need a different time? Just reply R and we'll call you."

Texting rules

Text reminders for an existing appointment are generally treated differently from marketing messages, but consent rules vary by situation. This is general information, not legal advice; ask an attorney how texting rules apply to your business, and see TCPA lead compliance for the basics. Offer an opt-out in your message flow either way.

What to do when someone doesn't show

  1. Wait 10 to 15 minutes past the start of the window. Traffic and wrong turns happen.
  2. Call once. If no answer, leave a short voicemail.
  3. Send a text right after: "We're at your address and no one's answering. Want to reschedule? Reply with a good time or call [number]."
  4. Offer a new time within 24 hours. Rebooking speed matters.
  5. Tag the reason in your system: no answer, cancelled late, got other quote, wrong decision-maker, address issue.
  6. Follow up using the cadence in lead follow-up sequence for contractors.

Don't scold. Most no-shows stay possible customers for a week or two. Treat the first miss as an easy mistake and the second as a signal.

Should you charge for missed estimates?

Free estimates are common, and a fee can lower bookings. Some shops charge for complicated diagnostics or when an appointment is cancelled with very little notice. If you consider a fee, check local rules, tell callers up front, and talk to an attorney. For most shops, tightening reminders and booking quality gets farther with less friction.

Track attendance, then act on it

Measure the percentage of booked estimates that actually happen, by source and by booker. A big gap tells you something specific:

  • Low attendance from one source means those callers were less committed. Compare it with your call-to-estimate rate.
  • Low attendance from one CSR means booking technique or follow-through needs coaching.
  • Low attendance for appointments booked more than three days out means you need faster slots or a mid-point reminder.

Frequently asked questions

Why do homeowners miss estimate appointments?

Usually because they forgot, got a faster quote elsewhere, can't reach you to reschedule, or never felt committed in the first place. Most no-shows trace back to how the appointment was booked.

How many reminders should I send?

Four touches cover most shops: an instant confirmation, a day-before message, a morning-of message and an on-the-way text. Add a call only for large or high-value estimates.

Should I charge a no-show fee for estimates?

Free estimates are standard in many trades, and fees can cost you bookings. If you're considering one, check local rules and talk to an attorney, and weigh it against simply improving reminders.

What should I do after a no-show?

Wait ten to fifteen minutes, call once, send a text, and offer a new time within 24 hours. Then mark the reason so you can see patterns.
