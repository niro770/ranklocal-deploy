Storm Season Lead Strategy: Get Your Capacity Ready Before the Storm Hits

Prepare for storm season by locking in crew capacity, phone coverage and an estimating routine before the first event, then scale call volume only to what you can inspect within a few days. Storm demand is a short, loud spike. This page sits under the what makes homeowners call a contractor guide and covers the operations side of that spike.

Why storms break normal lead plans

A normal week gives you a steady trickle of calls. A hail swath or a hurricane landfall gives you a month of calls in a few days, from homeowners who are anxious, comparing three or four contractors, and often waiting on an insurance adjuster. Most of those homeowners will hire whoever picks up, shows up and sounds like they know the process.

That means the bottleneck isn't lead supply. It's how fast you can answer and how fast you can put a person on a roof. Contractors who buy a surge of calls without extra capacity pay for conversations they can't convert. The claim-to-call journey is covered in insurance roofing claims and leads. This page is about what to have ready.

Know your storm type

Different events create different work, and your prep should match.

Event Typical damage Work that follows Timing pattern
Hail Dented or bruised shingles, damaged gutters, siding, soft metals Roof inspections, claim support, roof and gutter replacement Calls start within days, inspections run for weeks
Straight-line wind Lifted shingles, fence panels down, tree limbs on roofs Repairs, tarping, fence work, tree removal Fast burst of urgent calls, then repairs
Hurricane Roof loss, water intrusion, flooding, downed trees Tarping, drying out, roofing, siding, windows Long tail, material and labor shortages
Tornado Severe localized structural damage Rebuilds, large insurance projects Concentrated, often slow to start

Hail is the classic roofing opportunity. Wind keeps fence and tree companies busy. Hurricanes stretch demand across many trades for months, and local supply chains can lag. Check your own market's weather history and plan for the event type that actually hits your area.

Build capacity before the event

Crews

Decide now how you'd add production if you needed to double it. Options include sub crews you've already worked with, a short list of overflow partners, and a clear cap on the number of jobs you'll sell per week. The cap matters. Selling 40 roofs when you can install 12 a month creates months of angry customers waiting under tarps.

Have the conversation with your best crews before the season, not after the storm when everyone is booked.

Phones

Storm calls come at night, on weekends and in clusters. Your office will not be able to answer a spike on a normal schedule. Plan for:

  • Extended hours for the first one to two weeks after an event.
  • An overflow route: answering service, a second line to a trusted person, or rollover to cell. The after-hours call handling comparison lays out the trade-offs.
  • A short intake script so overflow staff capture address, damage description, whether there's active water coming in, and insurance status.
  • A texted confirmation to every caller who leaves a message.

If you want a person focused on turning calls into scheduled inspections, that's what an appointment setting service is for.

Estimating

Inspection capacity is the real limit. Before the season:

  • Train or hire at least one extra inspector so you aren't dependent on the owner.
  • Standardize your inspection report so photos and notes look the same every time.
  • Set up drone or ladder-assist protocols that fit your insurance coverage and local rules.
  • Preload your estimate templates and material pricing, and confirm supplier lead times.

A homeowner who gets a written, photo-backed inspection within 48 hours will remember it. One who waits two weeks has already signed with someone else.

Scale call volume to your capacity

Surge buying works only if you've done the math ahead of time. Here's a hypothetical. Say your two inspectors can complete about 10 inspections a day between them over the first 10 working days. That's roughly 100 inspections. If about 1 in 3 inspections turns into a signed job, that's around 33 jobs, and your crews need to be able to install them within the window you promised. If your crews can only do 20, the extra 13 signed jobs become a backlog, or you stop buying calls at roughly 60 inspections' worth.

The numbers here are illustrative, not benchmarks. Use your own close rate, inspector pace and crew output. The method is the point: work backward from production, not forward from lead supply. The capacity test for taking more leads goes through it in more detail.

Set your answering and follow-up rules

During a surge, write down the rules so everyone follows them:

  1. Answer live if possible. If not, call back within the same hour.
  2. Prioritize active leaks and structural danger. Offer tarping or temporary repairs first.
  3. Book an inspection time at the end of the first call, not "we'll get back to you."
  4. Send a confirmation text with the inspector's name and window.
  5. Follow up with anyone who got an inspection but hasn't signed within two or three days.

Storm chasing and ethics

Storm chasers have a bad reputation for a reason. Homeowners have been burned by out-of-area crews who take a deposit and disappear, or who do poor work and can't be reached for a warranty claim. Homeowners are wary, and local contractors benefit from being the opposite.

Practical guidelines for staying on the right side of it:

  • Be local and visible. Have a real address, a working phone number and reviews from past customers.
  • Don't pressure. Offer a free inspection and a written report. Don't knock on doors claiming the roof is destroyed before you've looked at it.
  • Don't act as the adjuster. Contractors can document damage and support a claim, but the claim decision belongs to the insurer. Rules on what a contractor can say or do in the claim process differ by state.
  • Check deductible rules. Waiving or absorbing a deductible is restricted in some states and can create problems for you and your customer. Ask your licensing board or an attorney before advertising anything about it. This is general information, not legal advice.
  • Be careful with deposits. Take reasonable deposits, tied to materials, and give clear written terms.
  • Keep your license and insurance current, and show them. Out-of-state contractors usually need to meet local licensing rules too. See contractor licensing and lead generation.
  • Stay after the storm. The contractors homeowners recommend later are the ones who answer the phone in month six.

If you do travel to a storm market, treat it as a business expansion, not a raid. Get licensed, set up a local number and commit to the warranty work.

After the surge

The spike ends. Some contractors then discover they've hired for a peak that won't return. Before you hire permanently, decide which additions are temporary (seasonal inspectors, overflow answering) and which are worth keeping. Track source, cost and close rate per call so you can decide whether the surge calls paid back. Frequently asked questions

When should a contractor prepare for storm season?

Before the first storm, not after. Crew commitments, phone coverage and estimating tools take weeks to line up, and the busiest calls arrive within days of an event.

Should I buy more leads right after a storm?

Only as many as your crews and estimators can answer and inspect within a few days. Calls you can't get to fast go to the next contractor on the homeowner's list.

What is a storm chaser in contracting?

A contractor who travels to a storm area for short-term work and often leaves once demand drops. Local companies win trust by staying reachable for warranty and follow-up work.

Can a contractor offer to cover a homeowner's insurance deductible?

Rules on deductible waivers and rebates vary by state and some states restrict them. Check with your state licensing board or an attorney before advertising anything about deductibles.
