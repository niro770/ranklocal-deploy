Answering Service vs In-House CSR: Cost and Conversion Trade-Offs for Contractors

Compare an answering service and an in-house customer service rep (CSR) by cost per booked estimate, not by the monthly bill. A service is cheaper in cash and flexible. A CSR usually books more estimates because they know your business and can use your calendar. Which wins depends on call volume and what one job is worth.

This is one decision inside how to answer contractor leads. All the figures below are hypothetical, built to show the method. Replace them with your own call volumes, wages, and close rates.

What each model actually is

Answering service: an outside team answers in your company's name. Depending on the arrangement, they take messages, run a short intake, transfer emergencies, or book into a calendar you share. You pay by month, minute, or call.

In-house CSR: an employee who answers, qualifies, books, confirms, and often handles scheduling and follow-up. You pay wages, taxes, benefits, equipment, and management time.

Third option: outsource booking to a team focused on it. Appointment setting is an existing RankLocal service for that, and it's worth a look if you don't want to hire.

The costs people forget

Monthly invoice versus loaded cost is where most comparisons go wrong.

Cost Answering service In-house CSR
Base Monthly plan or per-call fees Wage
Overage or extras Minutes beyond plan, after-hours rates, transfers Overtime
Taxes and benefits None Payroll taxes, insurance, paid time off
Setup Scripts, call routing Recruiting, training time
Equipment None Computer, headset, software seat
Management Periodic review Daily coaching
Coverage gaps Rare Sick days, vacation, lunch
Turnover None to you Rehiring and retraining

An in-house CSR's wage is only part of the cost. A common rough rule is to add a meaningful percentage for taxes and benefits, and that percentage varies by state and plan. Ask your accountant for your real figure rather than trusting a rule of thumb.

An illustrative cost comparison

Assume a contractor gets 200 inbound calls a month during business hours and after hours combined. These are hypothetical.

  • Service: $1,100 a month all-in.
  • CSR: $4,200 a month fully loaded, including wages, taxes, software, and a share of management time.

Cash difference: $4,200 - $1,100 = $3,100 more for the CSR.

Now the part that matters, conversion. Suppose:

  • The CSR books an estimate on 45% of calls: 200 x 0.45 = 90 estimates.
  • The service books on 30% of calls: 200 x 0.30 = 60 estimates.

Why might the service book fewer? They don't know your pricing context, can't always see your calendar, and often take a message that you still have to return, which adds delay. Your numbers may differ, and a good service with calendar access can close much of the gap.

Cost per booked estimate:

Model Monthly cost Estimates Cost per estimate
Answering service $1,100 60 $18.33
In-house CSR $4,200 90 $46.67

On cost per estimate, the service wins by a wide margin. That's the number many owners stop at. Now add what an estimate is worth.

What an extra estimate is worth

Suppose 30% of estimates close, and each job averages $2,500 in gross profit. An estimate is worth 0.30 x $2,500 = $750 in expected gross profit.

The CSR books 30 more estimates (90 - 60). That's 30 x $750 = $22,500 in expected gross profit. The extra cost is $3,100. The net advantage to the CSR is $22,500 - $3,100 = $19,400 a month under these assumptions.

Break-even is more useful. The CSR has to produce $3,100 / $750 = about 4.13, so roughly 5 extra estimates a month, to pay for the extra cost. In this example they produce 30. If the CSR only produced 3 extra estimates, the service wins.

That's why the question isn't "which is cheaper" but "how many more estimates does the dearer option book, and what's an estimate worth?"

When the service wins

Run the same math with different inputs:

  • Low volume: 40 calls a month. The CSR's fixed cost spreads over fewer calls. Even with a better booking rate, the extra estimates may not cover $3,100.
  • Low-value jobs: if an estimate is worth $150 rather than $750, you'd need about 21 extra estimates to cover $3,100 (3,100 / 150 = 20.7, so 21).
  • Mostly after-hours calls: a CSR who works 9 to 5 doesn't help with 8 p.m. calls.
  • Spiky demand: services scale instantly. A CSR can't be hired for storm week and fired after.
  • Hiring problems: if you can't hire and keep a good CSR, the model is theoretical.

When the CSR wins

  • Steady daytime volume: enough calls to keep one person busy.
  • High-value jobs: roofing, full HVAC replacement, and remodels, where one extra job covers months of cost.
  • Complex intake: trades where the right questions are specialized.
  • Calendar control: the CSR can see techs' routes and book realistic slots.
  • Follow-up capacity: a CSR can also chase unbooked leads and send reminders, which a message-taking service doesn't do. See lead follow-up.
  • Brand and tone: callers get someone who knows your company.

Conversion is where the money moves

The booking rate (calls that become estimates) is the lever. Track it for either model, and compare it to your own history. What is call-to-estimate rate explains the measurement. A 5-point gain on 200 calls is 10 more estimates a month. At $750 each, that's $7,500 in expected gross profit.

What moves the rate:

  • Speed of pickup. Fewer abandoned calls.
  • Live calendar access. Booking now rather than promising a callback.
  • A good script. See the intake script.
  • Coaching. Scoring calls and feeding back. See how to train a contractor CSR.

An answering service with calendar access, a tight script, and monthly call reviews can book close to what a CSR does. A CSR with no training and no script can book worse than a good service. Setup beats model.

Questions to ask before you sign or hire

If you're looking at a service, get plain answers to these first.

  • Do agents see my calendar, or do they only take messages?
  • Who writes and updates the script, and how fast can I change it?
  • How are emergencies escalated, and what's the maximum time before a human is reached?
  • Can I listen to call samples, and how often?
  • What happens on holidays and during storm surges, when volume spikes?
  • How is billing counted for short calls, transfers, and spam?

If you're leaning toward a hire, ask yourself different questions. Can I pay for a full month of training before the rep is productive? Who covers the phone during their lunch and vacation? Who coaches them weekly? A CSR with no coaching is the most expensive version of the in-house model, because you pay full wages for average booking.

A hybrid that fits many shops

  • CSR in business hours handles inbound and does outbound follow-up.
  • Service for overflow: when the CSR is on a call or at lunch, calls roll to the service.
  • Service or AI after hours. See after-hours call handling.
  • Emergency escalation goes to an on-call tech.

The hybrid costs more than either alone at low volume and often pays at higher volume, because you stop losing calls during peaks.

How to test before you commit

  1. Pilot the service for 30 days with a tracked number so you can see bookings by source.
  2. Compare to your baseline: booking rate before and after.
  3. Listen to 10 calls from the pilot. Score them. Would you hire that voice?
  4. Cost the CSR honestly with your accountant's loaded rate.
  5. Decide on cost per booked estimate and expected gross profit, not on the invoice.

If you're also weighing lead sources, cost per job is the right measure for them too. A call you've paid for and answered poorly costs the same as one answered well, so the front end determines what a lead source is worth to you.

Frequently asked questions

Is an answering service cheaper than hiring a CSR?

Usually in monthly cash, yes, especially at low call volume. But the cheaper option can cost more if it books fewer estimates, so compare cost per booked estimate rather than the monthly bill.

At what call volume should I hire an in-house CSR?

There's no fixed number. When the person answering your phones is also running jobs or quoting, and calls pile up during the day, a dedicated CSR starts to pay for itself, mostly through booked estimates.

Can an answering service book appointments?

Some can, if they have access to your calendar and a clear script. Many only take messages, which means you still do the callback, so ask exactly what the service does with each call.

Can I use both?

Yes. A common pattern is an in-house CSR during business hours and an answering service for overflow and after hours.
