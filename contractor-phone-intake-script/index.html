Contractor Phone Intake Script: Word-for-Word to Qualify and Book a Caller

This is a phone script you can print and tape next to the phone. It opens the call, captures contact details, qualifies the job, and books a specific estimate time, with branches for emergencies, price questions, and callers outside your area. Brackets mark the parts you fill in.

It's the working document behind how to answer contractor leads. Read the pillar for the reasoning, then use this page for the words. For the thinking behind the question order, see how to qualify a home service caller.

Rules before you read the script

  • Smile before you pick up. It sounds silly and it works on your voice.
  • Ask one question at a time. Two questions in one breath get half an answer.
  • Write while they talk. Use a form or CRM with the same fields every time.
  • Never say "I'll have someone call you" if you can book now.
  • Stay in control with questions. Whoever asks the questions steers the call.

Step 1: Greeting

"Thanks for calling [Company Name], this is [Your Name]. How can I help you today?"

Keep it under five seconds. No "your call is important to us." If it's after hours and you're covering a rollover line, use: "[Company Name], this is [Your Name], I'm covering the after-hours line. What's going on?"

Step 2: Capture contact details first

"Happy to help with that. Before we get into it, so I don't lose you if we get cut off, can I get your first and last name?"

"And the best number to reach you at?"

Read it back: "That's [number], right?" Then:

"What's the address where the work would be done?"

Branch: out of service area. If the address is outside your area:

"I appreciate you calling. We don't serve [city] right now, so I don't want to waste your time. If you'd like, I can suggest asking [a trade association or a company you trust] for a referral."

Only recommend a specific contractor if you know them well. Otherwise keep it general.

Step 3: Understand the problem

"Tell me what's going on."

Let them talk for up to a minute without interrupting. Then follow up with trade-specific questions. Examples:

  • Roofing: "Is it leaking now, or is this about age or damage?" "About how old is the roof?"
  • HVAC: "Is it not running at all, or running but not cooling or heating?" "How old is the system?"
  • Plumbing: "Is water actively coming out right now?" "Do you know where the shutoff is?"
  • Fence: "Is this a repair or a full replacement?" "About how many linear feet?"
  • Pest: "What are you seeing, and where in the house?" "How long has it been going on?"

Branch: emergency. If water is flooding, a gas smell, no heat in freezing weather, or a tree on the house:

"Okay, that sounds urgent. Here's what I want you to do right now: [safety step, such as shut the main water valve, leave the house for a gas smell]. I'm sending someone, and I need two quick details to get them moving."

For a gas smell or any danger to life, tell the caller to leave the building and call emergency services or the gas utility from outside. Then gather the address and number you already have and dispatch. Detail can wait.

Step 4: Confirm ownership and authority

"Are you the homeowner?"

If not:

"Got it. Will the owner be there for the visit, or are you authorized to approve the work?"

Landlords, tenants, and property managers all call. A tenant often can't approve a repair. That doesn't make it a bad lead, but you need to know who signs.

Step 5: Scope, timeline, and decision

"How soon are you looking to get this taken care of?"

Listen for the real answer. "Whenever" and "this week" are different calls.

"Have you had anyone else out to look at it?"

This one tells you whether you're first, second, or fourth. Don't treat the answer as a problem. It tells you how to pitch the visit.

"Is there anyone else who'll be part of the decision?"

If yes: "Great, let's pick a time when they can be there too."

Step 6: Budget, gently

Wait until after the problem is clear.

"To make sure I send the right person, have you set a budget for this, or are you still figuring out what it might cost?"

Both answers are fine. If they say still figuring it out, you've set up the estimate visit as the way to learn the number. If they name a figure far below what the job costs, don't argue, just note it for the estimator.

Step 7: Book it

"Here's what I'd suggest: have [Estimator Name] come out, take a look, and give you a firm price. It's [free / a flat fee of $X, if you charge]. I have [Day] at [time] or [Day] at [time]. Which works better?"

When they pick:

"Perfect, I've got you down for [Day, Date] at [time] at [address]. [Estimator Name] will call when he's about 30 minutes out. Is [number] still the best number for that?"

Then give prep instructions:

"Please have [access to the attic, the unit's model sticker, the area cleared]. Is there anything about the property we should know, like a dog or a gate code?"

Step 8: Close and confirm

"You'll get a text from us in the next minute confirming the time. If anything changes, just reply to it or call this number. Thanks for calling, [Name]. We'll see you [Day]."

Send the confirmation text before you start the next task. Reminders are covered in why estimate appointments no-show.

Branch: the price shopper

Caller: "How much does a new [thing] cost?"

"Fair question. It depends on a few things, mostly [size, access, materials]. Jobs like this usually run [range if you can give one]. I can't give you an exact number sight unseen, but [Estimator Name] can give you a firm price in about [time] at no charge. Would [Day] or [Day] work?"

Caller: "I'm just getting a few quotes."

"That makes sense, and you should. I'd rather you compare real numbers. Can I ask what matters most to you in picking someone, price, timing, or something else?"

Then act on the answer. For more lines, see how to handle price shoppers on the phone.

Branch: they don't want to book yet

"No problem. What would help you decide? I can send over a few details about us and check back with you [Day], would that be okay?"

Get permission for the follow-up, log it, and put a task on the calendar. Then use your follow-up sequence.

Branch: wrong service or too small

"I appreciate the call. That's not something we do, but [referral] might be able to help."

Be kind. Word travels in a town.

Branch: they ask for the owner

"[Owner] is out on a job right now. I handle scheduling and can book the visit myself, and I'll make sure he has all the details. Would [Day] or [Day] work?"

Make the script yours

Record yourself on three real calls and compare them against this page. Cut anything that sounds off. Add the trade-specific questions only your estimator knows to ask. Then train whoever answers with it, as described in how to train a contractor CSR. If you'd rather not staff the phone at all, appointment setting is the service that covers booking.

Frequently asked questions

Will a script make me sound robotic?

Only if you read it flat. Learn the questions and their order, then say them in your own voice. The order is the part that matters, and the exact wording can drift a little.

How long should an intake call take?

Most routine calls fit in three to five minutes. Emergencies run shorter because you dispatch first and gather detail on the way.

Should I ask about budget on the first call?

Ask gently, and late. A question like "Have you set a budget for this?" lands well after you've shown you understand the problem, and badly in the first thirty seconds.

What if the caller refuses to give their address?

Explain why you need it, which is to confirm you serve their area and send the right person. If they still hold back, take a name and number and offer a callback, but don't book a visit without an address.
