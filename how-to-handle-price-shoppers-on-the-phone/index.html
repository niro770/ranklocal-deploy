How to Handle Price Shoppers on the Phone: Objection Handling for "Just Getting Quotes"

When a caller says "I'm just getting quotes," don't argue and don't stonewall. Agree that comparing is smart, give an honest range or the factors that drive your price, ask one scope question, and offer the easiest next step. The goal is to be the contractor who sounded organized, not the cheapest voice on the phone.

This page covers one objection inside the broader guide to answering contractor leads. It assumes you've already started qualifying the caller and want to stay in the conversation when price comes up early.

What a price shopper actually is

A price shopper is a homeowner who leads with cost. That's most of them at some point. They've never bought a roof, a water heater or a fence. They don't know what's reasonable, so they collect numbers.

The caller usually falls into one of these types:

Type What they say What they need
The first-timer "Ballpark, what does a new AC cost?" Education, not a quote
The comparer "I've got two quotes, want a third" A reason to choose you besides price
The budget-bound "I can only spend X" An honest fit check
The tire-kicker "Just curious" A light-touch follow-up
The competitor or fishing call No address, only prices A polite exit

Treating all five the same wastes your time on some and loses the first-timers, who are often the best customers.

Principles before the lines

  1. Don't take it personally. Asking about price is normal.
  2. Never trash the other bids. You haven't seen them.
  3. Use ranges you can defend. Quote only what your estimators would stand behind.
  4. Shift from price to fit. Reliability, timing, warranty and communication are what comparers use to decide after the numbers look similar.
  5. Keep it short. A thirty-second answer that ends in a question beats a two-minute speech.

Sample lines by situation

Read these aloud. Replace the bracketed parts with your real numbers and terms. If a line doesn't sound like you, change it.

"How much does it cost?" (first sentence of the call)

"Good question, and I want to give you something useful. For a [job type], most homes land between [low] and [high]. What moves it is [two factors: size, age, access, material]. Can I ask a couple of quick questions so I can tell you where you'd probably fall?"

You've given a number, shown you understand the variables, and started qualifying.

"I'm just getting quotes."

"That makes sense. You should compare. To make your comparison fair, it helps if every contractor looks at the same thing. Can I ask what you've got so far, and what's most important to you, price, timing or warranty?"

This invites them to talk. The answer tells you what to emphasize.

"Can you give me a price over the phone?"

"I can give you a range, which is [range]. I don't want to give you an exact number without seeing it, because [a short real reason, such as hidden damage or unusual access], and a wrong number helps neither of us. The visit takes about [30 minutes], and you get a written quote within [time]. Want me to find a slot?"

If you do have flat rates, say so plainly and book.

"Another company quoted me less."

"I appreciate you telling me. I haven't seen their quote, so I can't compare it. What I'd suggest is lining up what's included: the materials, the warranty, who's doing the work and the permit. If the scope is the same and theirs is lower, take it. If it's different, let us walk through it and you can decide with full information."

Don't undercut on the phone. If you do offer discounts, do it after the estimate, when you know the real job.

"I have a budget of $X."

"Thanks for telling me, that helps. Let me ask a couple of questions to see if we can work inside that. If we can't, I'll tell you honestly before anyone drives out."

If the budget is below your minimum, say so with respect, and refer them if you can. "Our smallest jobs of that type start around [number]" is a fair thing to say.

"I just want a number, I don't want someone at my house."

"Totally understandable. Some people don't want a sales visit. Here's what I can do: send me three or four photos, and I'll give you a written ballpark by email today. If it looks like a fit, we can schedule a visit later."

Photo estimates work well for some trades and poorly for others. Offer only what you can do reliably.

"Why do I need an estimate? Can't you just tell me?"

"I wish I could, and for simple jobs I can. For this one, the price depends on what's behind the wall or under the shingles. The visit is free and takes about [time], and I'd rather give you a number I can stand behind."

Adjust "free" if you charge for estimates. Whatever you say, say it clearly.

"I'll call you back."

"Sure. So you don't have to hunt for my number, I'll text you my name and direct line. Is this a good number? And do you want me to hold a time on Thursday in case it helps?"

Then put them in your follow-up sequence. "I'll call you back" without a next step means the lead evaporates.

"I don't want to give you my address."

"No problem. I only need the address to confirm that we serve your area and to route the right person. If you'd prefer, give me just the zip code for now."

If they still refuse after a zip code and a service description, politely end the call. You can't give a price on nothing.

Giving ranges safely

Ranges help, but they can backfire. Follow these rules:

  • Base the range on real past jobs. Not on what you hope to charge.
  • Say what's included. A range for "a replacement" and a range for "supply and install with permit" are different things.
  • Label it as a range, not a quote. "Typical, not a quote."
  • Don't anchor too low. If your last ten jobs started at $8,000, don't say "starting around $5,000" to get a booking. You'll lose the person at the estimate anyway.
  • Keep it consistent. Everyone in your office should give the same range for the same job.

If you don't know your own typical numbers, pull the last twenty jobs and compute the low and high. That's a fifteen-minute exercise that makes your phone answers better.

Move from price to value

When the price question is out of the way, you want two or three differentiators ready, in plain words. For example:

  • Same-day or next-day estimates
  • A written warranty and what it covers
  • Licensed and insured, with proof available
  • A single point of contact
  • Photos and a written scope so the homeowner can compare

Say them once, in a sentence, then go back to booking. Don't recite a brochure.

When to book and when to walk

Not every call deserves an estimator's afternoon. Use a simple test.

Book it if: - You have an address in your service area - The caller described the job - They're willing to schedule within a reasonable window - The decision-maker will be there

Offer a lighter option if: - They're early in research, so send photos, a ballpark or an email - They're price-only but willing to talk, so give the range and tag for follow-up

Politely end the call if: - No address, no job description and only demands for a price - A fixed price demanded for unseen, variable work - Abusive or pressuring behavior

A calm exit line: "I don't want to give you a number I can't stand behind. If you'd like to share details later, we're here." Then move on.

Practice and review

Phone skills improve with repetition. Have your CSRs role-play the five types above, then compare recordings from real calls. A call scorecard with a line for "handled price objection without losing control of the call" turns this into coaching. Track the share of price-led calls that still end with a booked estimate as part of your call-to-estimate rate.

A note on lead sources

How much of your phone time goes to price shoppers depends partly on where the call came from. With shared leads, a homeowner may be getting several contractors calling at once, which pushes the conversation toward price. Quick reference card

  • Agree that comparing is smart.
  • Give a defensible range, then name what moves it.
  • Ask one scope question.
  • Offer a specific next step with two times.
  • Text your name and number.
  • Tag the lead and follow up.

Frequently asked questions

Should I give a price over the phone?

Only if your price is genuinely predictable, such as a flat-rate service call. For jobs that depend on what you find, give a typical range and explain what moves it, then offer the estimate.

What do I say when a caller only asks "how much?"

Acknowledge the question, give a range or the factors behind the price, then ask one scope question to keep the conversation going. Answering with silence or a flat refusal usually ends the call.

Is every price shopper a bad lead?

No. Most homeowners compare, and that's reasonable. Treat the call as a chance to show you're organized and trustworthy, and decide afterward whether the job fits.

When should I let a caller go?

When they won't share an address, refuse any description of the job, or want a fixed number for work that can't be priced unseen. A polite exit protects your estimators' time.
