CRM for Contractors: What to Look For Before You Buy

Pick a CRM by what your team will actually do with it: log every call, follow up on every open quote, and tie jobs back to the lead source. Judge ease of use on a phone, how calls and leads get in, reporting you can trust, and the real cost of leaving. This is a selection guide, so it names no products. It's one piece of growing a home-service company.

Start with the problem, not the software

A CRM holds who contacted you, what you said, what you quoted and what happened next. If that's already in an owner's head and a pile of texts, the cost shows up in three places:

  • Quotes that nobody followed up on.
  • Callers who dial back and nobody remembers them.
  • No way to say which source sent the jobs you sold.

Write down which of those hurts you most. That's your first requirement. Everything else is a bonus. Owners who skip this step buy for features they never use and then abandon the tool in a month.

The criteria that matter

1. Does your crew use it on a phone?

Estimators, techs and CSRs work from trucks, driveways and attics. If updating a record takes a laptop or ten taps, it won't get done. In a demo, make the person doing the demo log a new lead and move it to "quoted" on a phone, with a clock running.

2. How do leads and calls get in?

Manual entry fails first. Ask how each of these arrives:

  • Inbound phone calls, including the caller's number and the source number they dialed
  • Web form submissions
  • Texts
  • Leads from marketplaces or vendors, and how duplicates are handled
  • Missed calls

If the answer to "how do calls get in" is "you type them," treat that as a cost. For the tracking side, see call tracking for contractors.

3. Can it record the lead source properly?

A source field you can pick from a list is better than free text. Ask whether you can add your own sources, whether it keeps the original source after later contacts, and whether a repeat customer is tagged as repeat instead of overwriting the first source. Without this, you can't answer which leads became jobs.

4. Pipeline stages that match your process

Your stages might be: new call, appointment set, estimate done, quote sent, follow-up, won, lost. Make sure you can rename and reorder stages without paying someone. Check that "lost" needs a reason, such as price, timing, no response or went elsewhere. Lost reasons are some of the most useful data you'll collect.

5. Follow-up reminders and sequences

The most common failure in a contracting company is an open quote with no second contact. Ask whether the system:

  • Creates a task when a quote is sent
  • Reminds the owner of the person assigned
  • Sends texts or emails on a schedule, and how it handles a reply
  • Stops the sequence when the customer books or says no

Automations are only as good as the stop rules. A homeowner who booked and still gets "just checking in" texts will remember it. The cadence itself is covered in lead follow-up sequence.

6. Scheduling and dispatch

Some tools stop at the sales side and others also schedule crews, track jobs and send invoices. Neither is right for everyone. Decide whether you want one system or two that talk to each other, and ask how they sync. Two systems that don't sync means double entry, and double entry means stale data.

7. Reporting you can trust

Ask to see the reports in the demo, built from sample data. You want, at minimum:

  • Leads and calls by source
  • Appointments set and estimates done by source
  • Quotes sent, won and lost by source
  • Revenue by source
  • Time from first contact to first response

Then ask how a field turns into a report. If "won" is a manual click, your numbers are only as good as the clicks. This feeds your cost per job math.

8. Roles and permissions

An owner, an office manager, a CSR and a tech need different views. A tech probably shouldn't see margins. A new CSR shouldn't be able to delete a customer. Check whether you can limit access by role and whether former employees can be removed immediately.

9. Call notes, transcripts and summaries

Some systems attach recordings, transcripts or AI summaries to a contact. If you're considering that, check where the audio is stored, who can hear it, and how it's handled under your state's recording rules, which is a question for your attorney. How summaries can help coaching is covered in AI call scoring and summaries. Treat any summary as a draft that a person should spot-check.

10. Routing and assignment

If you run more than one crew, territory or estimator, ask how new leads get assigned: by zip code, by round robin, by job type, by availability. See multi-crew lead routing for how to design the rules before you buy a tool to enforce them.

11. Data ownership and exit

Before you sign, ask:

  • Can I export every contact, note and job in a standard file format, at any time, without a fee?
  • What happens to my data and phone numbers if I cancel?
  • Is there a minimum term, an auto-renewal or a notice period?
  • Who owns the tracking numbers?

A tool that holds your customer list hostage is a tax on every future decision.

12. Total cost

Price is rarely just the seat fee. Ask about:

  • Per-user pricing and what counts as a user (do part-time techs?)
  • Charges for texting, calling or phone numbers
  • Setup, training and data import fees
  • Add-on modules you'll need to get the features you were shown
  • What changes at renewal

Then add your own cost: the hours your office will spend setting it up and learning it.

Questions for the demo

  • Show me a new call arriving, from ring to record, with no typing.
  • Show me how a repeat caller is matched to an existing customer.
  • Show me what a salesperson sees when they open the app on a phone.
  • Show me the report that tells me which source sent my won jobs.
  • Show me how I'd cancel and what I'd get.
  • Who sets this up, how long does it take, and what do you need from us?

How to run a trial

  1. Pick two people who will use it daily, and the owner.
  2. Import a small, clean set of current leads, not your whole history.
  3. Run all new calls through it for two to three weeks.
  4. At the end, ask: Did anything get dropped? Did people use it without being nagged? Can I see where my jobs came from?

If the answer to the last question is no, the tool isn't doing the job you hired it for.

Mistakes that leave a CRM unused

  • Buying for the owner, not the people typing. If the CSR hates it, it dies.
  • Importing a messy list. Duplicates and dead contacts bury the live ones.
  • No owner of the system. Someone needs to be responsible for stages, sources and clean-up.
  • Too many fields. Every required field is a reason to skip the entry.
  • Starting with automations. Get the manual process right, then automate it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a CRM for a small contracting company?

If you can't say which open quotes need a follow-up today, you need a system, even a simple one. A spreadsheet works for a while, but it breaks once more than one person touches leads.

What is the most important CRM feature for a contractor?

Whether your team will use it on a phone in a truck. A simple system everyone updates beats a deep one that sits empty.

Should the CRM connect to call tracking?

Ideally yes, so each call lands as a record with its source attached. Ask in the demo exactly how phone numbers, calls and source tags get into the system.

How long should a CRM trial run before I commit?

Long enough to run real leads through it, usually a few weeks. Test with the people who will use it daily, not just the owner.
